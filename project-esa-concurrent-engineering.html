Workshop · ESA Academy, ESA-ESEC Belgium · October 2022

Designing a Venus mission in ESA’s Concurrent Design Facility

ESA Academy Concurrent Engineering Workshop 2022 · I was on the AOCS team.

ESA write-up of the workshop

The setting

Thirty university students from thirteen ESA Member States and Malta, four days, at ESA Academy’s Training and Learning Facility at ESA-ESEC in Belgium — which also houses ESA’s educational Concurrent Design Facility. Two ESA systems engineers and a systems engineering assistant from ESA’s Systems and Concurrent Engineering Section provided the tuition.

The point of the workshop is the method rather than the mission. In concurrent engineering, every subsystem is designed in the same room at the same time, so a choice made in one propagates immediately to all the others instead of surfacing weeks later in an integration review. Sessions ran in ESA’s CDF using the concurrent model-based design tool the facility works with.

The challenge: LOVE

On day one the teams were handed the problem: design a mission carrying both a spacecraft and a floating platform to Venus. Venus is close to Earth in size and density, which makes it a natural laboratory for how habitability evolved in the Solar System — and a punishing thermal environment. The students named the mission LOVE, for Large Orbiter Venus Explorer.

Three constraints did most of the work in shaping the design:

  • survive the thermal environment at Venus,
  • carry a large floating platform in addition to the orbiter,
  • fit inside the mass budget of a single Ariane 6.2 launch.

Nine teams covered configuration, structures and mechanisms, trajectory analysis, propulsion, AOCS, power, thermal, communications and data handling, and optics and sensors. I worked on AOCS — attitude and orbit control.

How it actually went

The scale of the problem showed up quickly. Reaching another planet with an orbiter and a floating platform put volume and mass under immediate pressure, so the trajectory and propulsion teams had to work through many options rather than settle on one early. Thermal was the other pinch point: Venus demands large radiators, and large radiators need somewhere to sit where they never see the Sun — a configuration problem as much as a thermal one.

The engineers pushed the teams through two full design iterations, assessing each subsystem’s needs and functions before consolidating, then refining everything in a second pass toward a converged design. The week ended with a presentation to ESA experts: the final design, the trade-offs behind it, and an honest list of what remained open.

What I took from it

The lesson that stuck is the one the method is built to teach: on a spacecraft, subsystems are not separable. An AOCS decision about actuators is also a power decision, a mass decision, and a configuration decision, and you find that out in minutes rather than months when everyone is designing in the same room. That coupling is the same thing I now work on formally — in hybrid systems, where the interaction between modes is exactly what makes the control problem hard.
